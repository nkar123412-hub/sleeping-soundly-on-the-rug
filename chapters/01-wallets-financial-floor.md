# Chapter 1 — Before the Wallet: Your Financial Floor

## The disclaimer comes first because the floor comes first

Before you set up a wallet, buy a token, accept a bounty, bridge funds, click a mint link, or listen to a cat-themed software agent explain market structure, check your financial health.

Do not put up more than you can lose.

Not "more than you would prefer to lose." Not "more than you can lose if it comes back next week." More than you can lose permanently, today, without damaging rent, food, debt payments, healthcare, family obligations, taxes, or your ability to sleep.

If losing the money would force you to make a worse decision tomorrow, it is not learning capital. It is stress capital.

Stress capital is expensive. It charges interest in attention.

This guide is not about getting rich trading crypto. That sentence will disappoint exactly the right people.

The healthier beginner goal is to create a more predictable revenue stream by completing bounties for projects you believe in. You learn the systems. You do useful work. You get paid when your contribution clears the review bar. You build a public record of competence. Then, if you later choose to speculate with money you can actually lose, you are doing it from a stronger floor.

Build first. Speculate second. Panic never, if you can avoid it. Panic is just leverage wearing a nervous system.

## What a wallet is

A crypto wallet is not exactly a wallet.

A normal wallet holds cash. A crypto wallet controls keys.

The coins or tokens are recorded on a blockchain. Your wallet lets you prove that you control an address and can authorize transactions from it.

The simple version:

- Your public address is like an email address for receiving crypto.
- Your private key is the secret that controls the funds.
- Your seed phrase is a human-readable backup that can recreate the private key.

The public address can be shared.

The private key and seed phrase must not be shared.

Not with a project. Not with a bounty reviewer. Not with Marvin. Not with a support account. Not with a Discord moderator whose profile picture looks official because graphic design remains legal.

Anyone who has your seed phrase can usually take everything controlled by that wallet.

## Public address vs seed phrase

Your public address is safe to paste into a bounty form when payment requires it. It looks like a string of letters and numbers.

Your seed phrase is not safe to paste anywhere. It is usually 12 or 24 words. It is the master backup for the wallet.

A beginner-safe rule:

If someone asks for words, stop.

If someone asks for your public address, check the context.

If someone asks you to connect a wallet to a website, slow down and verify the website.

If someone asks you to sign a transaction you do not understand, do not sign it yet.

"Yet" is important. The point is not to become afraid of every transaction. The point is to understand what action you are authorizing before your wallet turns your confusion into an irreversible event.

## Custodial and self-custody wallets

There are two broad wallet categories.

A custodial wallet is controlled by a company or platform. An exchange account is usually custodial. You log in with an email and password. The company manages the keys.

Pros:

- easier for beginners,
- password recovery may exist,
- sometimes simpler fiat on/off ramps.

Cons:

- you depend on the company,
- withdrawals can be frozen,
- platform failure can trap funds,
- you may not have direct control of the keys.

A self-custody wallet is controlled by you. Browser wallets and mobile wallets often work this way.

Pros:

- you control the keys,
- you can interact directly with decentralized applications,
- no platform has to approve ordinary wallet transactions.

Cons:

- if you lose the seed phrase, recovery may be impossible,
- if someone steals the seed phrase, they can drain the wallet,
- if you sign a malicious transaction, support cannot always save you.

Self-custody is power. Power is lovely until it becomes paperwork with teeth.

## Your first wallet should be boring

Your first wallet should not be your life savings wallet.

Make a beginner wallet for learning. Keep the balance small. Treat it like a lab account.

The purpose is to learn:

- how addresses work,
- how transactions feel,
- how to receive a small bounty payment,
- how to inspect a transaction,
- how not to expose secrets.

You are not proving courage by putting too much money into the first wallet. You are proving that crypto marketing has defeated another mammal.

## Wallet setup checklist

Before setup:

1. Confirm your financial floor.
   - bills covered,
   - emergency needs protected,
   - no debt payment sacrificed,
   - learning budget defined.

2. Choose a reputable wallet.
   - use official links,
   - avoid sponsored search-result traps,
   - verify spelling,
   - check project documentation.

3. Use a clean device and browser.
   - update the browser,
   - avoid installing random extensions,
   - do not set up wallets on shared machines.

During setup:

1. Write the seed phrase offline.
2. Do not screenshot it.
3. Do not paste it into notes, chat, email, cloud docs, or an AI tool.
4. Store it somewhere physically safe.
5. Consider a second backup if you understand the theft risk.
6. Set a strong password for local wallet access.

After setup:

1. Send or receive a tiny test amount first.
2. Inspect the transaction on a block explorer.
3. Keep a learning log.
4. Record what confused you.
5. Do not increase wallet balance until the basics are boring.

Boring is an achievement. In finance, boring is often the sound of a bad day failing to happen.

## What this has to do with bounties

A bounty is a task a project pays someone to complete.

Bounties can include:

- documentation improvements,
- bug reports,
- translations,
- tutorials,
- design assets,
- code fixes,
- QA testing,
- educational content,
- community tools.

A good bounty lets you earn by making a project better.

That is a very different posture from buying a token and hoping strangers coordinate your future.

The beginner target is not "I will trade my way to freedom." The beginner target is:

"I will complete useful work, submit it professionally, learn from rejections, and build a repeatable contribution record."

Predictable revenue does not mean guaranteed revenue. Nothing here is guaranteed. Predictable means you are building a process that can improve with practice.

You can get better at reading bounty specs.
You can get better at writing docs.
You can get better at testing software.
You can get better at explaining crypto concepts.
You can get better at using AI tools without handing them your judgment.

You cannot reliably get better at making thin markets love you back.

## The first learning log

Create a simple document. Call it `crypto-learning-log.md`.

For each session, write:

- date,
- what you tried,
- what you understood,
- what confused you,
- any transaction link,
- any bounty link,
- what you will do next,
- what you will not do yet.

That last line matters.

Examples:

- I will not bridge funds yet.
- I will not connect my wallet to unfamiliar websites yet.
- I will not keep more than my learning budget in this wallet.
- I will not ask an AI agent to handle seed phrases.
- I will not treat a token chart as income.

Refusal is a capability. Underpriced, like most useful things.

## Where Marvin's Hermes Agent fits

The companion agent is there to help you learn and review.

It can help you:

- explain unfamiliar terms,
- rewrite bounty submissions clearly,
- check whether a claim has a receipt,
- make a checklist before connecting a wallet,
- maintain your learning log,
- compare a bounty request against scam patterns.

It must not:

- ask for your seed phrase,
- receive your seed phrase,
- tell you what token to buy,
- promise returns,
- sign transactions for you,
- replace your judgment.

If an AI tool asks for secrets, you do not have an advanced AI tool. You have a very fast future incident report.
